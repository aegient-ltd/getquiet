How Quiet Protect works

A plain-English explanation of how Quiet Protect decides what to deliver, what to hold back for you, and what to block.

Why an AI, and not just a list of rules

The obvious way to stop scam emails is a list of rules: flag anything claiming to be HMRC, block links to lookalike websites, and catch urgent language. Rules are fast and predictable, but on their own they aren’t enough.

Scams are written to look genuine: the calm tone of a routine letter, a web address one letter off the real one, a request for bank details with no deadline. What matters is not whether a warning sign is there, but what it means in context. A payment request from a tradesperson who has written four times before is routine. The same request from someone who has never written before is suspicious.

So Quiet treats each email as a reasoning task. An AI model weighs what the email asks for against who sent it, their history with the person you protect, and the results of checks on where the email really came from. On top of that, fixed safety rules can only make the outcome more cautious, never less.

Five things it looks at

Every new email is assessed in five ways.

What it checks What it looks for
Pretending to be someone Claiming to be a bank, a government department, a delivery firm or a well-known online service; lookalike web addresses; a display name that doesn’t match where the email really came from
Pressure Deadlines, threats of fines or account suspension, false security alerts, and fear or guilt used to rush a decision
What it asks them to do Log in through a link, hand over bank or card details, send money to new bank details, pay by gift card or cryptocurrency, call an unknown number, or share personal details
How it is put together Links that go somewhere other than they say, QR codes in place of links, a reply address that differs from the sender, “Dear Customer” greetings, attachments from someone new
Tricks aimed at vulnerable people Prizes and inheritances out of the blue, fake computer support, a relative in trouble asking for money, unexpected romance, and any request to keep the email secret from family

Deliver, hold back or block

Every email ends up in one of three places. Safe email is delivered. Clear scams are blocked. Emails in between that are plausible enough not to block but suspicious enough not to deliver are held back for you to look at, rather than being forced into a yes-or-no decision by an AI.

Mistakes in a safety product are not equal. Holding back a genuine email is a nuisance. Delivering a scam can cost someone their savings. So when Quiet is unsure, it holds the email back. You know whether your parent was expecting a document from that sender. Quiet doesn’t.

Quiet also considers how sure it is. Near the line between delivering and holding back, or between holding back and blocking, an email Quiet is less sure about goes the more cautious way. Blocked emails stay in your dashboard too, so you can release one if Quiet got it wrong.

Rules that always apply

Whatever the AI concludes, some emails are always held back or blocked:

  • An email that fails the checks on its origin is held back, regardless of what it claims to be.
  • An email claiming to be from an organisation, such as a bank or a government department, is held back if Quiet can’t confirm it really comes from them.
  • A request to send a payment to new or changed bank details is held back, even if it comes from someone they know.
  • An email requesting bank details or a combination of personal details such as a National Insurance number, date of birth, and address is blocked.
  • A prize or inheritance from someone who has never written before is blocked.
  • An email made entirely of pictures from someone they rarely hear from, or with an attachment from someone new, is held back.

What you see, and what you don’t

When Quiet holds an email back, you get a short email alert. Your dashboard shows who sent it, when it arrived, and what Quiet decided and why, in Quiet’s own words. You also see which senders they trust, block, or hear from often; how often each one writes, and how often they reply; and how much marketing they receive.

You never see the subject line, the message or any attachment. That is deliberate. Quiet Protect exists to keep scams out, not to give anyone a window into someone else’s private letters.

When you release or block an email

Your decisions are recorded. Releasing one email doesn’t teach Quiet to let similar emails through: if you want a sender’s emails to arrive, mark them as trusted. Blocking a sender holds back their future emails.

What it can’t do yet

There are things Quiet can’t do yet, and we would rather say so plainly.

It won’t catch everything

No email check does. When Quiet isn’t sure, it holds the email back for you rather than guessing.

It doesn’t look inside attachments

Quiet knows an attachment is there and who sent it, but it doesn’t read what is inside. That is why attachments from someone new are held back for you.

Emails made of pictures are hard to check

Some scams are a single image, with no text for Quiet to read. Quiet holds these back when they come from someone they rarely hear from, but it can’t check what the picture says.

Microsoft email accounts only, for now

Quiet Protect works with Outlook.com, Hotmail, Live and Microsoft 365 accounts today. Gmail and Yahoo need Google’s and Yahoo’s approval first.
